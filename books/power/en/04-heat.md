# Heat

In winter, heat can be the difference between an inconvenient power cut and a home that eventually becomes unsuitable to occupy.

Heat preparedness does not begin with buying a heater. It begins with understanding **how the home is normally heated, what stops working without electricity, and how little of the home actually needs to remain comfortable**.

## 1. Map the normal heating system

Record which parts require electricity:

- direct electric heaters
- underfloor heating
- heat pump
- electric boiler
- circulation pumps
- controls and thermostats
- fans
- district-heating interface equipment
- pellet stoves or other systems with powered feed or fans
- wood-burning stove or fireplace

A heat source can use a non-electric fuel and still depend on electricity for pumps, fans or controls.

## 2. Reduce heat demand first

The cheapest preparedness energy is the energy you do not need to produce.

During a power cut:

- add clothing layers early
- use hats, warm socks, blankets, duvets and sleeping bags
- choose one or a few rooms as a **warm zone**
- close doors to unused rooms
- reduce unnecessary air exchange without blocking required ventilation
- use curtains and other normal measures that reduce heat loss without creating fire or moisture problems

The first objective is to keep people warm, not to recreate normal temperatures throughout the building.

## 3. Create a warm zone

Choose the warm zone before winter. A useful candidate normally has enough space for the household, is small enough to heat realistically, can accommodate any safe alternative heat source, is close to necessary functions, has working smoke detection and can be monitored with a thermometer.

Record temperatures during a safe test or a real outage. This teaches you how quickly your particular home cools.

## 4. Wood-burning stove

An approved wood-burning appliance and a sound chimney can provide heat independently of the grid.

Think of it as a complete system:

**appliance + chimney + dry fuel + ignition + tools + smoke detection + extinguishing equipment + routine**

Plan the wood supply in terms of actual operating time. Where practical, measure your own consumption under representative winter conditions rather than relying on a universal kilograms-per-day figure.

Use only fuels and firing methods for which the appliance is designed.

## 5. Portable gas or LPG heater

A portable gas heater is an alternative only when the appliance is **explicitly designed for indoor use**.

Ventilation, minimum room size, clearances from combustible material, supervision and the manufacturer's instructions all matter. Combustion consumes oxygen and produces carbon dioxide and water vapour; incomplete combustion can produce dangerous carbon monoxide.

Treat gas heating as a complete system:

**approved appliance + correct fuel and regulator + safe cylinder handling + ventilation + detection + supervision + fuel budget**

An LPG camping stove, portable outdoor cooker or patio heater does not become an indoor heater merely because it burns the same fuel.

## 6. Gas storage

How much LPG may safely and legally be stored, and where it may be stored, depends on the cylinder, building type and national or local rules.

This Europe-first edition therefore does not give a universal cylinder count. Record the endurance you need and the appliance's documented or measured consumption, then check the current rules for the country in which the equipment will be stored and used.

This is particularly important in flats, basements, storage rooms and shared areas.

## 7. Direct electric heating

Resistance heating requires large amounts of power and energy compared with lighting, phones and radios.

A battery system capable of supporting communications for several days can be depleted very quickly by an electric space heater. Any electric backup heating must therefore be budgeted explicitly in watts and watt-hours. A 230 V socket on a power station does not by itself make electric heating practical.

## 8. Heat pumps

A heat pump can deliver more heat than the electrical energy it consumes, but it still depends on electricity and its behaviour is model- and temperature-dependent.

For preparedness, establish:

- electrical input under relevant conditions
- starting or peak demand where relevant
- performance at the winter temperatures you are planning for
- compatibility with the intended generator or inverter
- behaviour during very low temperatures and faults

Do not size backup power from an advertised annual efficiency figure alone.

## 9. Hydronic and district heating

Even where heat comes from district heating, wood, gas or another source, the dwelling may depend on circulation pumps, valves and controls.

Find out what **your** installation needs. Backing up a relatively small circulation pump may be much more realistic than producing the heat electrically, but use measured or documented figures for the actual installation.

## 10. Pellet and other automated systems

Pellet stoves and other fuel-burning systems may need electrical feed mechanisms, ignition, fans and controls. Fuel-burning does not necessarily mean grid-independent.

Include these electrical dependencies in the energy budget.

## 11. Smoke and carbon monoxide

Working smoke detection is fundamental. Where combustion equipment can create a carbon-monoxide hazard, follow the current guidance for the appliance and jurisdiction and provide appropriate safety layers.

An alarm does not make unsafe appliance use safe. It is an additional warning layer, not permission to use outdoor equipment indoors.

## 12. House versus flat

### House

A house may offer a wood stove, chimney, larger fuel storage and more alternative systems, but its larger area may also mean greater heat loss. Do not automatically try to heat the whole building.

### Flat

A flat may lose less heat through external surfaces and neighbouring dwellings, but can offer fewer alternative heat sources and tighter restrictions on fuel and combustion appliances.

The plan should therefore put particular emphasis on clothing and sleeping equipment, a warm zone, battery power for small loads and a pre-arranged warm place to relocate to.

## 13. When should you relocate?

There is no single indoor-temperature threshold appropriate for every person and situation.

Consider whether anyone is unable to stay warm, the needs of children or older and vulnerable people, relevant medical needs, the rate at which the building is cooling, expected outage duration, whether safe heating remains available, water and frost risks, travel conditions and the availability of alternative accommodation.

Define decision points **before** conditions become difficult.

## 14. Test the heating plan

A safe winter test can record outdoor temperature, starting indoor temperature, warm-zone temperature over time, rooms closed off, heat source, actual fuel or energy use, battery use, ventilation, comfort and problems.

Do not disable essential heating in a way that risks people, animals, plumbing or the building merely to make an exercise realistic. A safe partial test is better than creating a real emergency.
