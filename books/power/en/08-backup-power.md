# Backup Power

Backup power begins with needs, not products. Make three lists: **must work**, **should work** and **comfort**.

## W and Wh

**Watts (W)** describe how much power a load requires at a particular moment. **Watt-hours (Wh)** describe energy used over time.

A 10 W load operating for 5 hours ideally uses:

10 W × 5 h = 50 Wh

A 100 W load operating for 4 hours uses:

100 W × 4 h = 400 Wh

This distinction is the basis of the energy plan.

## Measure before buying

For every important load, record:

- measured or documented power
- hours per day or actual duty cycle
- whether a motor or compressor creates a starting peak
- whether the load genuinely needs 230 V AC
- how many days it must be supported

Measurements from the actual equipment are preferable to generic tables. A refrigerator, for example, does not necessarily consume its rated running power continuously.

## From load to daily energy

| Load | Power | Use | Energy |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| LED light | 5 W | 5 h | 25 Wh |
| Router | 10 W | 8 h | 80 Wh |
| Radio | 3 W | 4 h | 12 Wh |
| Phone charging | measured/estimated | per charge | record it |

Add watt-hours, not merely watts. Then decide which loads can be removed if the event lasts longer than expected.

## Three energy levels

### Level A — USB/DC

Phones, small lamps, radios and some network equipment can often operate directly from USB or DC. This can avoid unnecessary inverter losses.

### Level B — battery bank or power station

When several loads or 230 V equipment must operate, larger battery storage becomes relevant. Compare usable capacity with the daily energy budget rather than with the product's advertised power figure.

### Level C — generator or other generation

Large or long-duration loads may require energy production in addition to stored energy. Treat generators and solar as energy sources with constraints, not as unlimited electricity.

## Operating priorities

Define at least three modes:

1. **normal preparedness operation** — planned critical loads
2. **conservation** — essentials only
3. **minimum operation** — communications, necessary lighting and other genuinely critical needs

The Ploos preparedness tools should make these scenarios comparable while keeping assumptions visible.

## Electric vehicle as an energy reserve

An electric vehicle can contain far more stored energy than a normal power bank or portable power station, but usable backup capability depends on the vehicle and approved equipment.

### V2L — Vehicle-to-Load

Where the vehicle supports V2L, it can supply suitable equipment through the manufacturer's intended system. Follow the vehicle's instructions and output limits.

V2L supplying individual loads is not the same architecture as supplying a building's normal electrical installation. Do not improvise back-feeding or household-wiring connections.

### V2H — Vehicle-to-Home

V2H integrates the vehicle with a building system capable of supplying selected circuits when the grid is unavailable. This requires compatible vehicle and charging equipment, safe isolation from the public network and a correctly designed and installed electrical system under the applicable rules.

Treat these as separate arrangements:

**V2L to individual equipment ≠ dedicated backup installation ≠ V2H to a building**

### Energy budget

Where V2L or V2H is genuinely supported, record:

- battery energy the household is willing to allocate
- maximum permitted output power
- minimum state of charge reserved for driving
- priority loads
- intended operating time
- how the vehicle can be recharged

The vehicle remains a means of transport. Do not automatically allocate the entire traction battery to household loads.
