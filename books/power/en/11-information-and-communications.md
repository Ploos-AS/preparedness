# Information and Communications

During a prolonged power cut, information can become as important as electricity. You may need to know what happened, how large the affected area is, what authorities are advising and how to contact other people.

Do not build the plan around a single channel.

## 1. Use layers

A resilient information plan can combine:

1. **local observation and neighbours**
2. **broadcast radio**
3. **mobile phone**
4. **internet when available**
5. **agreed contact points and paper backup**
6. **additional receive-only radio for interested users**

Each layer can fail without removing every source of information.

## 2. The mobile phone

A phone performs many jobs: calls, messages, internet access, alerts, maps, camera, torch, authentication and payments. That makes its battery valuable.

Enable power saving early, reduce unnecessary screen use, use a separate lamp instead of the phone torch where possible, disable functions you do not need, avoid unnecessary streaming and keep the appropriate charging cable and power bank available.

A powered phone is more useful than one exhausted as a torch during the first few hours.

## 3. The mobile network

A charged phone does not guarantee an indefinitely functioning mobile network. Base stations, transmission links, backup power and traffic load may all be affected.

The plan is therefore not “I have a phone, therefore I have communications”. It is **phone + alternative information channel + agreed routines**.

## 4. Short messages

When networks are under pressure, short messages can be more practical than long calls or data-heavy services.

A household can agree a compact format such as:

**OK / location / need / next contact**

For example: `OK – home – all well – next contact 20:00`.

Do not distribute location or sensitive information more widely than necessary.

## 5. Internet and the home network

Internet access can disappear even when your router has power. Conversely, some connections may continue if local equipment receives backup power.

Map the ONT or modem, router, switches, Wi-Fi access points, their power consumption and which devices actually need to remain on.

Low-power network equipment can be a good candidate for DC, UPS or battery operation, but test whether the upstream service actually survives a local power cut. Do not spend scarce battery energy maintaining a home network that no longer has an external connection.

## 6. Broadcast radio

A battery-powered broadcast radio provides an information path independent of the household internet connection.

The appropriate broadcast system, stations and official emergency-information arrangements vary by country. Before relying on it, identify the current official advice for your jurisdiction and test the actual receiver: can everyone use it, are batteries or charging available, is it usable in the dark, and does the household know which trusted sources to seek?

## 7. Paper backup

Keep essential information outside the phone:

- important telephone numbers
- household contact plan
- meeting point
- address of alternative accommodation
- relevant local contacts
- necessary medical information where appropriate
- a simple overview of preparedness equipment

Paper needs no battery, password or network.

## 8. Contact plan

Decide who contacts whom, who checks on children or people needing assistance, where to meet, what to do if the mobile network is unavailable, when to try again and whether an out-of-area contact can act as a coordination point.

Keep the plan short enough to use.

## 9. Neighbours

A power cut is also a local event. Neighbours may help establish whether only one building or a wider area is affected, and households can share observations.

This does not replace household preparedness with somebody else's equipment. It adds a social information channel to the technical ones.

## 10. ATS-20 and other receive-only radios

For an interested user, a wide-coverage receiver such as an ATS-20 or ATS-20+ can provide access to additional radio signals, including shortwave reception.

In this book it is **additional receive capability**, not the primary preparedness radio for Europe.

It can use little energy and provide useful experience of how reception varies with time, frequency and antenna. It also requires more knowledge than a normal broadcast receiver, reception varies, and received information may be unverified.

A dedicated ATS-20 title can later cover antennas, bands, modulation, frequency planning and practical listening exercises in depth.

## 11. Receiving is not transmitting

This book distinguishes clearly between **receiving** radio signals and **transmitting**.

Technical capability does not by itself establish permission to transmit. Transmitters, amateur radio and other two-way radio systems require jurisdiction-specific treatment and belong in dedicated material with current regulatory review.

## 12. Communications energy budget

Communications can be a relatively energy-efficient preparedness function.

Record phones, power banks, broadcast radio, ONT or modem, router, switches and any additional receivers, then calculate how long minimum communications can be maintained.

## 13. Information hygiene

During an incident, rumours can spread quickly.

Distinguish your own observation, information from a competent public authority, established editorial sources, reports from neighbours or acquaintances, and unverified online posts or received radio signals.

An interesting post or radio signal is a **data point**, not automatically a fact.

## 14. Minimum mode

When energy becomes scarce:

1. retain at least one phone
2. retain one independent receive channel
3. use agreed contact windows
4. switch off network equipment that provides no useful connection
5. conserve energy between information checks

The objective is not constant connectivity. It is the ability to **receive important information and reach people when necessary**.
