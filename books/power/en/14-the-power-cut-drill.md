# The Power-Cut Drill

A plan that has only been read has not been tested. The power-cut drill is intended to expose poor assumptions while normal electricity, shops, communications and help are still available.

Keep the exercise controlled. You do not need to disconnect the home from the grid. Leave safety-critical, medical and other equipment that must remain continuously available operating normally. Instead, simulate an outage by agreeing which normal conveniences will not be used.

## Before the drill

Choose a duration and scope. A first exercise might last two to four hours one evening. Later, try a full evening, overnight period or day. Over time, consider both a winter and a summer exercise.

Agree the start and stop time, participants and exceptions. Record outdoor temperature and other conditions that may affect the result.

Do not create a hazard for realism. Do not rewire electrical installations, close necessary ventilation or test combustion equipment in a way that departs from safe operation.

## Start: the first minutes

If the household is comfortable with it, begin without announcing the exact starting second. Find emergency lighting without using normal lights. Follow the routine from the first-minutes chapter.

Measure how long it takes to find lighting, the paper plan and other priority equipment. If someone has to search in darkness, record that as a finding.

## Test information and communications

Work out how you would obtain outage information without relying on every normal channel at once. Test a radio if it is part of the plan. Locate important telephone numbers on paper.

Send the agreed concise status message. Also test charging a phone from the backup source the plan actually assumes.

## Test lighting and daily routines

Use only preparedness lighting. Carry out normal movement between the living area, bathroom and entrance. Check stairs and other places with a fall risk.

Record which lamps are actually used, their brightness settings and whether battery or charge levels are adequate.

## Prepare one preparedness meal

Make a meal using the method and food assumed by the plan. Do not improvise with equipment that is not intended for the place where it is being used.

Measure roughly how much water, energy or fuel the meal consumes. Notice utensils, openers, lighting, washing-up and waste — small dependencies often become visible only when the meal is actually prepared.

## Test the heat plan

In cold weather, you can simulate minimum operation by spending most of the exercise in the planned warm zone and reducing ordinary electric comfort heating where this can be done safely.

Record temperature at the start and at regular intervals. The aim is not to make the home dangerously cold, but to learn how quickly conditions change and whether clothing, room choice and the backup heat plan are realistic.

## Test energy and water

Connect only loads for which the backup-power system has been planned and safely configured. Record actual power or energy use if the equipment can display it. Compare this with the energy-budget worksheets.

If the home depends on a pump or another powered water supply, simulate the limitation without harming the system. Measure how much water the household actually uses during the exercise.

## Observe people, not only equipment

Record what is difficult to find, understand or remember. Can everyone use the lights? Does more than one person understand the plan? Are instructions readable in poor light? Does unclear responsibility create unnecessary stress?

These findings matter as much as battery capacity and watt-hours.

## Finish with a debrief

At the end, record findings before returning to normal routines. Divide them into four groups: **missing**, **worked poorly**, **worked well** and **needs investigation**.

Then update the plan, worksheets and stores where appropriate. A shopping list alone is not a completed debrief; better placement, labelling, training or simpler procedures are often the right fix.

## Passing the drill

The drill passes when the household can find the plan and critical equipment, meet the agreed needs safely, record real consumption and explain when it would move to reduced operation or relocation.

Finding a weakness is not failure. A drill that reveals a problem before a real outage has done its job.
