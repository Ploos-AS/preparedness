# Light

Good emergency lighting does more than make a room visible. It reduces falls, lets you read information and operate equipment, and helps preserve a normal daily rhythm. The aim is not to illuminate the whole home as usual, but to put useful light where it is needed while consuming little energy.

## First choice: light without combustion

Head torches and battery lamps are the first choice. A head torch leaves both hands free for cooking, checking a consumer unit, tending a stove and other practical tasks. A diffused battery lamp is better suited to general room lighting.

Keep more than one independent light source. A lamp can be misplaced, discharged or fail. It is useful to distribute lighting between the entrance, living area and the place where preparedness equipment is stored.

## Know the energy source

Record which batteries the lamps use and whether they can run from ordinary replaceable cells, a rechargeable battery, USB or several alternatives. Standardise on fewer battery types where practical so that stocks are easier to rotate.

For rechargeable lighting, know roughly how long it lasts on low and high settings. The lowest useful brightness can provide much greater endurance than maximum output.

A simple energy budget is **power in W × hours used = Wh**. Four 3 W lamps used for five hours each evening nominally require 60 Wh per evening. That is often a small load compared with heating or cooking, but it becomes significant over several days.

## Put light where you need it

Keep a light where you can find it in darkness. Do not store every torch in the same drawer. Stairs, entrances, bathrooms and places with trip hazards deserve particular attention.

Avoid using the phone as the primary torch. It is an important communications resource, and continuous screen and LED use consumes energy that may be needed later.

## Candles and open flames

Candles and other open flames add fire risk and should not be the default when battery lighting is available. If an open flame is used, never leave it unattended or place it where children, animals, textiles or an unstable surface can lead to a fire.

Smoke alarms and accessible firefighting equipment must remain effective during the outage. A power cut is not a reason to accept lower fire safety.

## Night mode

Decide in advance what minimum lighting the home needs overnight. One dim orientation light in a strategic place may be more useful than switching on several bright lamps whenever someone gets up.

In a flat, communal areas, stairs and access systems may be darker or behave differently from normal. Keep personal lighting available before leaving the flat.

## Test the plan

A simple test is to turn off normal lighting for an evening and use only the preparedness lighting. Check whether you can find the equipment in darkness, move safely, prepare simple food, read necessary information and complete the evening routine without wasting energy.

If the test requires you to find a charger first, buy batteries or use the phone all evening, you have identified a concrete improvement.
