# From 72 Hours to Seven Days

This scenario connects the systems in the book. It is not a prediction of how a power cut will unfold. It is an exercise in making decisions while information, energy and other resources change.

## 0–15 minutes — do not overreact

1. Establish safe lighting.
2. Determine whether only the dwelling or a wider area is affected.
3. Check breakers or residual-current protection if this can be done safely.
4. Note when the power failed.
5. Keep the fridge and freezer closed.
6. Put the phone into power-saving mode.
7. Check whether anyone has an immediate need.

Do not start the generator, empty batteries or begin cooking a hot meal merely because the electricity has been off for five minutes.

## 15–60 minutes — establish the situation

Use the available information channels. Establish, where possible, expected duration, weather and temperature, water supply, mobile and internet service, people who need contacting, heating needs and medical or other critical requirements.

Choose an initial operating mode.

## 1–6 hours — protect resources

### Energy

Switch off loads that do not need backup power. Preserve energy for lighting, communications and other priorities.

### Food

Keep refrigeration closed and prefer food that does not consume unnecessary energy.

### Heat

In winter, add clothing early and establish the warm zone before the dwelling becomes cold.

### Water

Confirm whether normal supply still works. A working tap is not a reason to forget the stored reserve.

## 6–12 hours — move into preparedness operation

If the event appears likely to continue:

- activate the planned cooking arrangement
- assess refrigeration using measured temperature and qualified food-safety guidance
- begin the planned generator/battery cycle where relevant
- check the warm zone
- check water and wastewater systems
- perform the first agreed communications window
- record consumption of energy, water and fuel

## 12–24 hours — the first day

By now the household should have some evidence of how quickly resources are actually being consumed.

Recalculate:

**remaining resource / actual daily consumption = approximate endurance**

Do this for drinking water, battery energy, generator fuel, heating fuel, cooking fuel, shelf-stable food and essential medical or other supplies.

If the plan no longer lasts long enough, enter conservation mode before resources become scarce.

## Day 2 — establish a rhythm

Long-duration preparedness becomes easier with routines.

**Morning:** information, temperature, water and energy status.

**Day:** necessary tasks, planned generator operation if applicable, food and maintenance.

**Evening:** communications window, next-day plan, lighting and heating arrangements for the night.

Avoid spending energy on continuous news monitoring.

## Day 3 — 72 hours

Seventy-two hours is a useful review point, not the end of the plan.

Review people and health, indoor temperature, water, food, hygiene, battery, fuel, information, payments, sanitation, weather and the expected duration.

Make an explicit choice:

**continue normal preparedness operation / conservation / minimum operation / relocate**

## Days 4–5 — endurance

Actual consumption now matters more than the original theoretical estimates.

Protect resources that cannot easily be replaced. Combine tasks where this saves scarce fuel—for example, a planned generator period that both supplies a necessary high-power load and recharges the battery.

Do not wait for the final litre of fuel, final piece of firewood or final percentage of battery before changing mode.

## Days 6–7 — the long week

At the end of the week the household should still have a safety margin rather than having deliberately planned to reach exactly zero.

Also consider what happens **after day seven** if normal services have not returned. Seven days is a planning horizon, not a promise that an incident ends on the seventh day.

# Scenario A — house, winter

A typical priority order is:

1. people
2. warm zone
3. water
4. information
5. critical electrical loads
6. food and refrigeration
7. other comfort

With wood heat, battery storage and a generator, functions can be separated:

**wood → heat | battery → small loads | generator → high-power loads + charging**

A well pump, circulation pump or other house-specific load can substantially change the plan.

# Scenario B — flat, winter

A typical priority order is:

1. people
2. clothing, sleeping equipment and warm zone
3. stored water
4. communications
5. battery power for small loads
6. food with little or no cooking dependency
7. early assessment of an alternative warm location

Do not build the plan around a generator, outdoor cooker or fuel store that the actual property cannot safely or legally support.

Relocation can be part of the primary plan rather than a failure of preparedness.

# Scenario C — summer

Space heating is usually less important, while refrigeration, food safety, drinking water, ventilation or overheating and fire risk during outdoor cooking may become more important.

Solar can be useful but remains variable generation. Use the same decision model as in winter with different priorities.

# After the event

When electricity returns:

1. check equipment before returning to normal operation
2. replenish water, food, batteries and fuel
3. perform required maintenance
4. record what actually worked and what did not
5. update the worksheets and household plan

A real power cut provides valuable test data only if its lessons are written back into the plan.
