# Food, Water, Fridge and Freezer

A useful preparedness pantry contains food the household already eats, in quantities it can rotate. During a power cut there is an additional requirement: the food must fit the cooking equipment, stored water and fuel that are actually available.

## Think in meals, not cans

Plan seven days as meals. Mark each meal as:

- **0 — no cooking:** ready to eat
- **1 — minimal cooking:** brief heating or hot water
- **2 — normal preparedness cooking:** requires a cooker and a meaningful amount of fuel

Keep enough level 0 and 1 meals that the household is not dependent on a working cooking location at every mealtime.

## Example building blocks

These are examples, not a universal nutrition plan:

- crispbread, long-life bread or an equivalent staple
- spreads, tins and other foods that can be eaten without heating
- nuts, dried fruit and other energy-dense foods the household likes
- oats or another simple breakfast
- soups, stews or foods requiring only brief heating
- precooked or canned pulses
- food for children and other specific needs
- food and water for pets

Adapt the plan for allergies, diet, medicines where food matters, and the household's actual needs.

## A simple seven-day model

Rather than depending on 21 elaborate emergency recipes, choose a small set of meals that can be repeated: a simple breakfast, a cold lunch, a no-cook main meal, a low-cook hot meal and a complete no-cook reserve.

The important principle is to retain at least one workable route through the week even when the cooking equipment cannot be used.

## The water budget includes food

Cooking water comes from the same finite reserve as drinking water. Record drinking water, water consumed as part of food, cooking water and other water required for hygiene and practical needs.

National preparedness recommendations differ, so the Europe-first edition keeps the calculation separate from the jurisdiction-specific recommended quantity. Use the applicable current authority guidance for the country concerned.

## The fuel budget includes the menu

Do not write “one gas cylinder” without knowing what it must accomplish. For each hot meal record the appliance, fuel, approximate consumption, cooking time and portions.

Test representative meals and measure real consumption where practical. Wind, cold, cookware, lids and appliance design can materially affect the result.

## LPG and other gases

LPG can be a useful preparedness fuel for suitable cooking equipment. Fuel type alone does not determine whether an appliance is safe indoors. Portable camping stoves and small gas burners should be used only in locations and conditions for which the appliance is explicitly designed and in accordance with current local safety guidance.

## The fridge and freezer are the first food store

Keep doors closed as much as practical and decide which food should be used first if temperatures begin to rise.

Use thermometers. Follow the labelled storage requirements for perishable foods and current food-safety guidance in your jurisdiction. How long a particular food remains safe outside its intended cold chain depends on the food, temperature, time and microbiological risk.

This book therefore does not invent a universal “discard everything after X hours” rule.

## Summer

Higher ambient temperatures can make refrigeration losses more urgent. Keep more meals that tolerate room-temperature storage and do not depend on saving the contents of the fridge or freezer.

## Winter

A balcony or outdoor area is not automatically a controlled refrigerator. Temperature can vary, food can freeze, animals may reach it and sunlight can warm containers. If outdoor temperature forms part of the plan, protect the food hygienically and monitor its actual temperature.

Outdoor winter cooking can also require more fuel and a cooking location that remains safe in wind, snow and darkness.

## Rotation

Treat the preparedness pantry as a buffer in the normal food system:

**buy → store → use → replace**

Mark a practical rotation order and review stocks regularly. A store of food the household never eats is harder and more expensive to maintain.
