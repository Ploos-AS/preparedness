# Example Profiles

These profiles are not prescriptions. They show how dwelling type, season and available equipment change the plan.

## Flat — winter

Prioritise warm clothing and sleeping equipment, a small warm zone, lighting, phone/radio and battery power. Keep a high proportion of meals that require no cooking.

If there is no safe and permitted outdoor cooking location, the plan cannot depend on a camping stove. Agree in advance where the household can relocate if the dwelling becomes too cold.

**Energy architecture:** small USB/DC loads → power bank/battery. Avoid spending scarce battery energy on electric space heating unless the system has genuinely been sized for it.

## Flat — summer

Space heating is normally less critical while refrigeration and food spoilage can become more important. Battery storage can support small loads efficiently. Shelf-stable food requiring little or no cooking adds substantial resilience.

**Energy architecture:** battery → lighting, communications and selected small loads. Any solar arrangement must suit the dwelling and the applicable practical and regulatory conditions.

## House with wood heat — winter

A suitable wood-burning appliance can help keep the dwelling habitable without grid electricity when the appliance, chimney, dry fuel and safety equipment are in order.

Choose a warm zone. Suitable outdoor cooking equipment can supplement the plan, while battery energy is reserved for lighting, communications and other selected electrical needs.

**Energy architecture:** wood → heat; battery → small electrical loads.

## House with wood heat + battery + generator — winter

This is the book's main hybrid example.

1. **Wood heat** keeps the warm zone habitable.
2. **Battery storage** supports lighting, phones/radio and selected small loads while the generator is off.
3. **Generator** operation is limited to planned periods for high-power loads and battery charging.
4. **Fridge/freezer** operation follows temperature and food-safety planning rather than automatically requiring continuous generator operation.
5. **Fuel** is budgeted from measured or documented consumption at a relevant load.
6. **Minimum mode** reduces both battery and fuel consumption if the event lasts longer than expected.

This creates useful functional separation:

**wood → heat | battery → endurance and quiet operation | generator → power and replenishment**

A failure in one subsystem therefore need not remove every essential function.

## House without wood heat — winter

Here the heating plan becomes the critical difference.

An alternative heater explicitly approved for indoor use may be appropriate when operated correctly, but outdoor cooking equipment is not a substitute indoor heater.

If safe independent heat is unavailable, relocation becomes more important. Sizing a small preparedness generator for ordinary electric space heating can produce a very different power and fuel requirement from supporting lighting, communications and refrigeration.

## House with generator — winter

Define the critical loads before sizing the generator. Operate it safely outdoors and include fuel, maintenance and realistic load testing in the plan. Battery storage can cover quiet periods between generator runs.

Do not plan to run “the whole house as normal” without calculating power, energy, starting loads and fuel consumption.

## House — summer

Generator or battery power may be prioritised for refrigeration, a water pump and communications. Solar can make a substantial contribution when conditions are favourable, but the food and fuel plan should still work when solar production is poorer than hoped.

## Add a private well

A house with a private well gains another critical dependency. The pump's normal and starting requirements must fit the intended power source. Pressure-vessel drawdown can provide a limited buffer, but should be measured rather than inferred from the vessel's nominal size.

Stored drinking water remains part of the plan even when backup power for the pump exists.

## Add an electric vehicle

Where a vehicle and approved equipment support external power, the traction battery may provide a large energy reserve for suitable loads.

Keep mobility as a deliberate reserve. Decide how much state of charge may be allocated to preparedness and how much must remain for transport. Treat appliance-level vehicle-to-load supply, a dedicated backup installation and vehicle-to-home operation as distinct architectures.

Do not improvise a connection into household wiring.

## The point of the profiles

Choose the profile closest to the real dwelling and replace its assumptions with measurements and documentation from your own systems.

The objective is not to buy everything mentioned in the book. The objective is for each necessary function to have a considered primary solution and, where failure has serious consequences, a realistic fallback.
