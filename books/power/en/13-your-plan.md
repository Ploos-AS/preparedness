# Your Plan

Equipment becomes preparedness only when the household knows how it will be used. The plan should therefore describe decisions, priorities and limits rather than merely becoming a shopping list.

Keep a paper copy in a known place. The phone may be out of power when the plan is most useful.

## 1. Describe the household and home

Record who the plan covers, including children, older people, animals and anyone dependent on medical or powered assistive equipment. Note the building type, floor, important shared services and which part of the home could serve as a warm zone.

Map normal heating, backup heat, a safe cooking location, water supply, sanitation and the electrical loads that are genuinely critical.

## 2. Define minimum operation

Decide in advance what must work if power is absent for one day, three days and seven days. Separate needs from conveniences.

For each critical function, the plan should answer: What does it require? How long can it operate? What is the fallback? What happens as that fallback approaches exhaustion?

This is particularly important for heat, drinking water, medical equipment, communications, lighting, food and necessary transport.

## 3. Assign responsibilities

A simple plan should say who normally does what. One person may follow outage information while another checks heat and fuel, but everyone in the household should know where lighting, water, first-aid supplies and the paper plan are kept.

The plan must also work if the person who normally knows most about electricity, heating or technical equipment is away.

## 4. Define decision points

Use three clear levels:

**Remain:** the home is safe, critical needs can be met and resource consumption is sustainable.

**Reduce consumption further:** duration or supply is uncertain, so the household moves to minimum operation to preserve heat, water, energy or fuel.

**Relocate:** the home can no longer be kept safe, necessary medical or hygiene needs cannot be met, or another concrete hazard makes remaining unreasonable.

Write thresholds in terms of observable conditions. “When things get bad” is difficult to act on. “If we can no longer keep the agreed warm zone usable” is a real decision point.

## 5. Plan where to go

Have at least one realistic alternative and agree it in advance. Record the address, contact method, transport option and what must be taken. Do not assume that a car, lift, garage door, public transport or payment systems will operate normally during a prolonged outage.

## 6. Plan communications

Agree how household members will contact one another if mobile data is unreliable. Keep important telephone numbers on paper. Decide who outside the household can act as a contact point if family members are in different places.

## 7. Match the plan to the stores

The plan and the stores must agree. If the plan assumes seven days, assess water, food, medicines, batteries, fuel and other consumables against that period. If a resource lasts only one day, the plan should say what happens on day two.

Use the worksheets in this book to make assumptions measurable rather than relying on memory.

## 8. Test and revise

A plan that has never been exercised contains unknown assumptions. Run the power-cut drill in the next chapter and record what did not work.

Update the plan when the household, home, vehicle, heating system or important equipment changes. Put the date of the last test and next review on the paper copy.

A good plan need not be complicated. It should be clear enough for another household member to follow in darkness, under time pressure and without access to this book.
